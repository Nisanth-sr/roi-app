# AI ROI Middleware — Founder Decision Brief

*What we're building, what we decided, and why it's the right call for where you are right now — not where you'll be in a year. Plain language throughout. For the document your engineers actually build from, see the companion file: `technical-architecture-spec.md`.*

---

## The headline decisions

1. **Build one application, not a network of services.** In architecture terms this is called a "modular monolith" — one codebase, one thing to deploy and pay for, but organized internally so pieces can be split out later without a rewrite.
2. **Use Next.js (a JavaScript framework) running on Node.js for both the website and the backend logic.** This is your team's comfort zone — no new language to learn.
3. **Use Supabase for the database, login system, and tenant security.** It bundles Postgres (the actual database), authentication, and row-level security (the database-enforced guarantee that Company A can never see Company B's data) into one service, for about $25/month.
4. **Host the application on Vercel**, the company that builds Next.js — the two fit together with the least friction. Roughly $20/month to start.
5. **Don't build the live proxy, the background job queue, or any direct connections to AWS Bedrock or Google Vertex yet.** Those exist to serve a version of the product ("Path A" in the original brief) that you're not building for at least 90 days. Building them now means paying engineering time and hosting cost to maintain infrastructure nothing is using.
6. **Total baseline infrastructure cost: roughly $45–50/month** — comfortably under the $500/month point where it's worth reconsidering this approach.

Everything below is the reasoning behind each of these, in plain terms, so you can defend every one of them in a room without an engineer translating for you.

## What you're building

Small companies that wrap AI models into their own product — content generators, AI writing tools — charge customers a flat monthly fee but pay their AI provider based on actual usage. Right now they can't see which of their own customers is quietly costing more than that customer pays. You're building the tool that shows them: revenue per customer minus AI cost per customer equals real margin per customer, instead of a company-wide average that hides the problem.

This is real, not hypothetical: one production AI company's gross margin reportedly swung from 36% down to negative 14% after a single customer's usage outgrew what their pricing accounted for. That's the exact scenario your product is built to catch before it happens.

## The shape of the 90-day build — and why it's smaller than the original brief's diagram

The original brief drew a component diagram (Section 5) that includes an API Gateway, a separate Auth Service, a live Proxy Service, a background job queue, and dedicated connector code for AWS Bedrock and Google Vertex. Every one of those exists to support the *live* version of the product — one that sits in the middle of a real-time AI request and has to respond in under 50 milliseconds. That's a real thing to build eventually. It is not what the first 90 days needs, and the brief's own Section 6 already says so.

What the actual 90-day MVP needs is much smaller:

- **One application** that accepts a spreadsheet (CSV/JSON) upload of AI request logs, accepts a spreadsheet of client revenue, applies pricing rules to compute cost, and shows a dashboard
- **One editable pricing table**, changeable by whoever's playing product manager without an engineer touching code — AI provider prices change monthly, sometimes weekly, so this has to be a settings screen, not a number buried in code
- **One database**
- **A job that rolls daily numbers into a monthly per-client summary** — it lives inside the same application, not as a separate service

That's the whole system. No gateway, no queue, no proxy, no live connection to Bedrock or Vertex at all. This version of the product never calls the AI providers — it reads logs that your customer already generated by calling them.

**Why this isn't a cost-cutting call:** even with unlimited budget, building a live proxy nobody's using yet doesn't buy you anything except something new to maintain and secure before you have a single real signal it's needed. This is about matching what you build to what the business can actually prove right now.

## The stack, and why

**Framework: Next.js + Node.js.** Next.js is a JavaScript framework that can handle both the customer-facing dashboard and the upload/API logic in one project — one thing to deploy, one repo, fewer moving parts for a small team. Since your team's strength is JavaScript/Node, this has close to zero learning curve. The trade-off: if the future live-proxy piece (Path A) needs to be a long-running, always-on process — which it will — that piece won't live inside this same Next.js deployment. It becomes its own small service later. That's fine; it's the seam we designed on purpose.

**Database, auth, and tenant security: Supabase.** Supabase is a managed version of Postgres (a database) that also includes a ready-made login system and built-in support for row-level security. Using it instead of hand-building login and access-control code saves real engineering time — time that matters more than the roughly $25/month it costs, given your 90-day window. The trade-off: you're relying on Supabase's infrastructure rather than running your own, which is the right trade at this size and would only become a real constraint at meaningfully larger scale.

**Hosting: Vercel.** Vercel is built by the same company that builds Next.js, so deployment is close to zero-configuration. One important detail worth knowing: Vercel's free tier explicitly prohibits commercial use, so this needs to be on a paid plan (~$20/month for one seat) from day one, not the free tier — it's a real product, not a side project, and the platform's own terms require the paid tier accordingly.

None of these three choices are the only valid answer — the technical spec lists the real alternatives your engineers considered for each and why they were set aside for now. If your team disagrees with any of them once they're in the details, that's a conversation worth having before code gets written, not after.

## What you're deliberately not building yet — and exactly when that changes

| Not building now | Why it can wait | Build it when... |
|---|---|---|
| Live proxy (Path A) | No customer has asked for real-time numbers yet; CSV upload proves the core idea first | A handful of paying customers specifically ask for real-time instead of monthly upload |
| Background job queue | Only needed to protect a live request from slowing down — nothing here is a live request | Same trigger as the proxy above |
| API Gateway | Rate-limiting and traffic shaping matter once external systems call you live; 5 known pilot customers uploading files don't need it | Same trigger as the proxy above |
| Direct AWS Bedrock / Google Vertex connections | Only needed if you're calling the providers on the customer's behalf, which Path B never does | Same trigger as the proxy above |
| Deciding whose cloud credentials you hold (customer's vs. your own) | This question only exists once you're proxying live calls | Same trigger as the proxy above |
| On-prem / self-hosted deployment option | No current prospect has asked for it | The first time a real prospect makes it a dealbreaker |
| Stripe/Paddle billing integration | CSV revenue upload is enough to prove the margin insight | Once you're past pilots and want revenue data to sync automatically |

Every one of these is cheap to add later because none of it was silently baked into the current design — they're additions, not rewrites.

## The one place we're not cutting corners

Everywhere else in this build, "keep it simple now, upgrade later" is the right call and it's cheap. **Tenant isolation is the one exception.** That's the database-level guarantee that one customer company can never see another's data — and here, it gets built correctly starting with the very first database migration, not after real customers' revenue numbers are already sitting in an unprotected table.

The reason this one doesn't wait: retrofitting isolation after real financial data already exists in production is a materially bigger, riskier job than building it in from the start, and this entire product's pitch depends on being trusted with a customer's most sensitive number. This is a trust call, not a speed or cost call — it's the one decision in this document where "do it right immediately" beats "keep it simple and revisit."

## Risks worth watching (none of these block starting)

- **Your first pilot customers may not actually have client-tagged logs to export.** The whole CSV-upload plan assumes your customer's own system already records which AI request belongs to which of their clients. Many small AI wrapper companies haven't built that yet — ask directly, before building the upload flow around the assumption: *"Can you send me that file right now?"* If the answer's no, that's a reason to help them add a few lines of logging, not a reason to build the live proxy early.
- **Database backup and recovery expectations aren't yet pinned down.** The default is Supabase's standard automatic daily backups — normal for a pre-revenue SaaS, no extra cost. If you've already promised any customer something firmer than that, say so; it changes the hosting conversation.
- **This category isn't empty.** Tools like Langfuse, Datadog's LLM observability add-on, and Braintrust already track AI cost per customer today. None of them combine that cost with your tenant's own revenue to produce a margin number aimed at a non-technical buyer — that framing, not the token-counting, is the actual wedge. Worth making sure the dashboard leads with margin and red flags, not raw token counts, since that's the one thing the general-purpose tools don't do out of the box.

## What happens next

1. **Ask your first pilot customer for a sample log export this week** — before a line of upload code gets written. This is the single fastest way to find out if the core assumption holds.
2. **Hand the technical spec to your engineers** and have them flag anything in it that conflicts with something they already know that isn't captured here.
3. **Get the Data Processing Agreement in front of a lawyer early** — you're handling other companies' revenue and usage data from day one; this isn't optional and isn't something either of us should draft.
4. **Revisit this document at the 90-day mark**, or the moment a paying customer asks for real-time numbers instead of monthly upload — whichever comes first.
